# AI Governance in M&A

**The frameworks, policies, and practices that organizations adopt to deploy AI in M&A processes responsibly — addressing confidentiality, bias, ethical standards, regulatory compliance, and user education.**

## Summary

As AI adoption in M&A accelerates, responsible governance has emerged as a distinct practitioner concern alongside efficiency gains. Bremen (2024) articulates a six-component governance framework developed through WTW's practitioner network and Fortune 500 roundtable discussions. The framework treats governance not as a compliance afterthought but as a foundational requirement for sustainable AI adoption in high-stakes deal contexts. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]

The six components are: (1) understanding how AI tools work (not just their purpose, but their operational mechanisms and limitations); (2) educating users and leaders on AI benefits, risks, and responsible usage; (3) establishing ethical usage standards covering privacy, copyright, source attribution, and information responsibility; (4) keeping confidential deal information safe — including preventing proprietary data from entering AI training pipelines; (5) addressing algorithmic bias in both input data and model outputs; and (6) understanding and complying with jurisdiction-specific regulatory and liability frameworks. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]

Confidentiality is a particularly acute concern in M&A contexts because deal teams handle highly sensitive, market-moving information. The risk that deal data could be incorporated into AI training data or exposed through cloud-based AI systems requires specific data handling protocols beyond standard NDA coverage. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]

## Variations / sub-concepts

- Confidentiality and data security in AI-assisted M&A
- Algorithmic bias in target identification and valuation

## Key claims across sources

- Responsible AI governance in M&A requires six components: understanding AI mechanics, user education, ethical usage standards, confidentiality protection, bias mitigation, and regulatory/liability compliance. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]
- Preventing proprietary deal information from being exposed through AI learning and training systems requires specific policies and processes — beyond standard M&A confidentiality procedures. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]
- AI governance frameworks must account for highly varied country- and regulatory-specific rules, with protocols to prevent and quickly address cross-jurisdictional compliance issues. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]
- Effective leaders began investing in AI governance (skills training, data safeguards) immediately when generative AI proliferated — proactive governance provides a competitive advantage. [[2026-05-04-bremen-2024-ai-accelerates-ma]]
- The Responsible AI framework requires that fairness, model explainability, accountability, transparency, privacy, ethics, and security/safety be addressed jointly — a model that is explainable but not fair, or transparent but not secure, does not qualify as Responsible AI. [[2026-05-04-arrieta-et-al-2020-xai-concepts-taxonomies]]
- Model explainability (XAI) is the core enabling pillar of Responsible AI: without it, fairness cannot be audited, accountability cannot be assigned, and regulatory compliance cannot be demonstrated. [[2026-05-04-arrieta-et-al-2020-xai-concepts-taxonomies]]
- Finance is explicitly identified as a high-stakes domain where XAI/Responsible AI is most critical — alongside medicine, law, and defence — because opaque AI decisions in these domains cannot be justified, legitimated, or regulated. [[2026-05-04-arrieta-et-al-2020-xai-concepts-taxonomies]]

## Key claims from Johnson, Pasquale & Chapman (2019)

- Regulators should not merely "fix" black-box AI in finance but should evaluate whether to formally limit or ban the use of algorithms in consumer credit markets where bias cannot be mitigated through transparency requirements alone. [[2026-05-07-johnson-pasquale-chapman-2019-ai-ml-bias-finance]]
- Algorithmic bias governance requires an industrial policy that steers underwriting technologies toward forms capable of being regulated and reformed — without such incentives, equal-opportunity values will be further marginalized. [[2026-05-07-johnson-pasquale-chapman-2019-ai-ml-bias-finance]]
- Accurately identifying sources of [[algorithmic-bias]] in credit decisions is as critical to risk management oversight as predicting default and prepayment risks — framing bias detection as a risk-management imperative, not merely an ethical one. [[2026-05-07-johnson-pasquale-chapman-2019-ai-ml-bias-finance]]
- Coordinated state-federal regulation — with states acting as laboratories of democracy — is the appropriate governance structure for ADM in financial services; federal preemption by the OCC removes the most nimble enforcement actors. [[2026-05-07-johnson-pasquale-chapman-2019-ai-ml-bias-finance]]
- The lack of algorithmic transparency "may lead to increasing information asymmetries between the financial institution and the individual" — embedding a direct link between black-box ADM and the information asymmetry problem central to M&A theory. [[2026-05-07-johnson-pasquale-chapman-2019-ai-ml-bias-finance]]

## Key claims from Herbosch & Mertens (2025)

- The selection, training, and supervision of an AI tool — not the accuracy of its output — are the decisive legal questions when AI errors occur in M&A; existing legal standards (duty of care, fiduciary duty, business judgment rule) already impose governance obligations without requiring new frameworks. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Explainability is not merely a technical preference but a legal safeguard: using an unexplainable AI model for an economically significant M&A decision risks (a) being found grossly negligent in Delaware and (b) failing the German Ision-doctrine plausibility review, because neither court can be satisfied that the board's informational basis was adequate. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Service providers (law firms, consultants) deploying AI in M&A due diligence retain full professional obligations and cannot contractually disclaim the duty to supervise AI output; the Belgian Flemish Bar Association's guideline (that lawyers need not disclose AI tool use to clients) does not eliminate supervision obligations. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- The EU AI Act's standards for high-risk systems (Articles 13–15: transparency, human oversight, accuracy) serve as an informal governance benchmark for M&A AI tools even when those tools technically fall in the low-risk category. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Residual governance failure risk ultimately falls on shareholders of acquiring companies — who have limited ex ante tools to constrain how the board uses AI in deal preparation. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]

## Key claims from Sele & Chugunova (2024)

- Human oversight is not automatically protective: human-in-the-loop design increased preference for algorithmic recommendations, but participants did not reliably correct the least accurate recommendations. [[2026-05-07-sele-chugunova-2024-human-in-loop-adm]]
- For M&A due diligence, governance should therefore define concrete review duties, escalation rules, and error-checking responsibilities rather than merely requiring that a human remains "in the loop." [[2026-05-07-sele-chugunova-2024-human-in-loop-adm]]
