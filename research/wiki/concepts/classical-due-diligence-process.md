# Classical Due Diligence Process

**The pre-AI, human-led approach to M&A due diligence as codified in practitioner literature: a multi-disciplinary, project-managed investigation spanning up to 16 functional domains, conducted between heads of terms and deal completion, with outputs feeding into price negotiation and post-acquisition planning.**

## Summary

The classical DD process, as defined in Howson (2003), rejects the common misconception that DD is a chore to be delegated to accountants and lawyers. Instead, it frames DD as an exercise in project management that must be properly planned, staffed across functional experts, and explicitly connected to post-deal integration. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]

Howson's model places DD in Stage 2 of a four-stage acquisition process (Stage 1: strategy/search/approach; Stage 2: heads of terms + DD; Stage 3: sale/purchase negotiation + completion; Stage 4: post-completion). While formally a Stage 2 activity, Howson's central argument is that DD outputs — especially on synergies and integration risks — must shape Stage 4. Pre-acquisition DD "should be constructed to give as good an insight as possible into post-deal implementation as to isolating pre-deal risks." [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]

A proper classical DD programme has five strands: (1) verification of assets and liabilities; (2) identification and quantification of risks; (3) contractual protection against those risks (feeding into negotiations); (4) identification of synergy benefits; and (5) post-acquisition planning. The fifth strand is the most frequently neglected in practice. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]

Howson's two tables in Chapter 1 together list 15 DD areas: Table 1.1 covers the three "main" areas (financial, legal, commercial); Table 1.2 covers 12 "other" disciplines (HR & culture, management, pension, tax, environmental, IT, technical, operational, IP, property, antitrust, insurance/risk). The book has 16 chapters, of which 13 are discipline-specific (Chapters 4–16); property and operational DD are subsumed within legal and financial chapters respectively rather than receiving dedicated chapters. The common shorthand "16 Disziplinen" merges chapter count with discipline count and overstates the number of independently treated areas by three. The correct description for academic use is "up to 15 DD areas covered in 13 discipline-specific chapters." For each area, standard checklists (14 in total, Appendix A) define the minimum information requirements. The breadth of this coverage is what distinguishes a "proper" from a "quaintly traditional" (legally and financially biased) DD programme. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]

Lajoux (2024) complements Howson's project-management model with a legal and securities-law anchoring: classical DD is grounded in both common law (the duty of care evolving through case law) and statutory securities law (the Securities Act of 1933 Section 11 "due diligence defense"). The four core DD activity streams — financial statements review, management/operations review, legal compliance review, and document/transaction review — map onto Howson's multi-discipline structure from a different professional vantage point. Lajoux (2024) additionally foregrounds cybersecurity, GDPR compliance, and cultural DD as substantive new classical-DD dimensions for the 2020s, while also situating DD formally in Phase 5 of a circular eight-phase M&A lifecycle (Strategy → Valuation → Financing → Structuring → Due Diligence → Negotiation → Closing → Integration). [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]

Bhagwan, Grobbelaar & Bam (2018) reinforce the breadth point through a systematic literature review: DD areas include financial, legal, tax, environmental, operational, market, HR, cultural, strategic, marketing, IP, technology, and R&D. This provides an academic support source for the same baseline that Howson establishes in practitioner literature. [[2026-05-07-bhagwan-et-al-2018-systematic-review-dd-ma]]

## Key claims across sources

- DD must be planned and managed as a project; it is "essentially an exercise in project management" rather than a legal or accounting exercise. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- There is no standard legal definition of DD in most jurisdictions; the lawyer's view focuses on confirming the buyer gets what it thinks it is buying, while the dealmaker's view frames DD primarily as a risk-reduction and price-negotiation tool. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- DD programme design should be calibrated to M&A type: Bowyer's five categories (overcapacity consolidation, geographic roll-up, product/market extension, R&D substitute, industry convergence) each call for a different investigative emphasis. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- The Cadbury Report (1992) recommended that UK boards review all significant acquisitions against a formal checklist — the closest UK governance equivalent to a formal DD obligation, though a soft "comply or explain" standard rather than a statutory requirement. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Vendor due diligence (VDD), commissioned by sellers, is a procedural variant that introduces an inherent framing risk buyers must guard against through supplementary own-side review. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- The classical DD process has a structural bias toward financial and legal aspects, which Howson (2003) argues causes acquirers to miss soft and operational risks — the same bias that enables broad AI automation of document review while leaving strategic, cultural, and management DD untouched.
- A 1999 KPMG study found an 83% correlation between post-deal integration implementation success and overall acquisition success, validating the argument that DD must feed PMI planning rather than stopping at deal close. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Synergy evaluation is the single most important hard key to deal success (+28% above average) per KPMG (1999) — reinforcing the argument that DD's synergy identification strand (strand 4) is underweighted in traditional practice. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- The true acquisition failure rate is probably around 75%, driven primarily by insufficient planning and over-focus on financials rather than by failures in the DD process itself. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Skeletons found in DD normally become negotiation points rather than deal breakers; the primary value of DD for the dealmaker is identifying issues that justify price adjustment or contractual protections, and providing comfort to proceed. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Advisers (lawyers, accountants, management consultants) tend to work in silos during DD, each covering only their own domain; effective DD programme management must bridge these silos. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Classical DD quality depends heavily on process governance: Howson's working-with-advisers chapter shows that unclear mandates, weak reporting lines, and poor adviser information-sharing can cause known risks to disappear before they reach the buyer's decision process. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- Cost pressure is itself a quality risk in classical DD: Howson argues that adviser reputation, relevant experience, and standing should outweigh simple fee minimisation because a cheap report can be worse than no useful report. [[2026-05-07-howson-2003-due-diligence-critical-stage]]
- AI tools (Jang & Stikkel 2024; Wang et al. 2023) currently automate only the legal and financial document-review components of DD — roughly Chapters 4 and 5 of Howson's 16-chapter framework — leaving management, HR, commercial, environmental, IP, and other domains entirely human-led. [[2026-05-04-jang-stikkel-2024-nlp-llms-due-diligence]] [[2026-05-06-wang-et-al-2023-maud-merger-agreement-understanding]]
- A systematic review of DD literature finds the field lacks clear guidance on which DD investigations to conduct, how to prioritise them, and what investigation depth is sufficient for risk minimisation. [[2026-05-07-bhagwan-et-al-2018-systematic-review-dd-ma]]
- Classical DD failure can occur after information discovery: acquirers may find negative information but fail to revise bids or withdraw because they treat noisy DD signals as insufficiently reliable. [[2026-05-07-puranam-et-al-2006-dd-signal-detection]]
- The legal foundation of classical DD in the US rests on the "due diligence defense" under Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933: directors and officers can defend against strict liability for registration statement misstatements by demonstrating a reasonable belief based on reasonable investigation. This anchors DD not just as a commercial practice but as a legal obligation. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Classical DD formally begins with signing of a confidentiality agreement and letter of intent, but preliminary review using publicly available information (press reports, SEC filings, offering memoranda) begins from the moment a buyer senses a possible opportunity — spanning the Strategy, Valuation, Financing, and Structuring phases. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- The level of DD required varies by company type (public companies trigger more extensive DD obligations than private companies) and transaction type (stock purchases trigger more DD responsibilities than asset purchases). [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Cybersecurity due diligence is now a substantive classical DD domain: the review must cover IT infrastructure, cloud applications, software tools, the IT/cybersecurity organisation, social media use, and GDPR compliance — dimensions absent from Howson (2003) but codified in Lajoux (2024) as standard practice. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Cultural due diligence is explicitly required in classical practice: the acquirer must assess the target's values, team structures, and motivations, with integration planning starting as early as possible during the DD phase. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Classical DD does not formally end at signing: the "bring-down" condition in most acquisition agreements obligates the seller to confirm no material adverse change has occurred through closing, extending the DD discipline up to and through deal completion. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
