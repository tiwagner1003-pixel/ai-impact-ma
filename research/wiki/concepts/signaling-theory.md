# Signaling Theory

**A theory explaining how information-advantaged parties reduce information asymmetry by taking observable, costly actions that credibly communicate their unobservable quality to information-disadvantaged parties.**

## Summary

Signaling theory originates with Spence (1973), who showed that costly actions (like educational credentials) can function as credible signals of unobservable quality because they are more affordable for high-quality types than low-quality types. Akerlof (1970) identified the market failure that signals resolve: without credible signals, adverse selection causes markets to collapse or price at the average quality, driving out high-quality sellers. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

In management research, signaling theory is the third most frequently used theoretical perspective on information asymmetry (11% of 223 reviewed articles per Bergh et al. 2019). It appears most prominently in research on IPOs (underwriting quality, auditor prestige, VC backing as signals), alliances, and firm reputation. The theory frames information asymmetry as a "lack of perfect information" problem and positions the signal as a resolution. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

In M&A, signals are critical in target evaluation and deal negotiation. A target firm's reputation, track record, and the prestige of its advisors and board members all function as signals to prospective acquirers. IPO underpricing itself is a cost that high-quality firms pay to signal their value; analogously, target firms that accept contingent earnout structures signal confidence in their future performance. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

## Variations / sub-concepts

- Observable signal (costly, credible) vs. unobservable quality (private information)
- Separating equilibrium (signals successfully distinguish quality levels) vs. pooling equilibrium (signals fail to differentiate)
- [[adverse-selection]] — the problem signals are designed to solve
- [[information-impactedness]] — signals reduce transaction costs driven by informational parity gaps

## Key claims across sources

- Signaling theory accounts for 11% of the 223 management articles reviewed by Bergh et al. (2019), making it the third most prominent theoretical lens. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- Signals — costly attributes providing information about otherwise unobservable qualities — clarify how market participants distinguish seller quality (Spence 1973, per Bergh et al. 2019). [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- IPO firms build coalitions of high-status actors (reputed VCs, prestigious underwriters, auditors) to vouch for their quality and constitute a credible signal to prospective investors. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- Reputation is a signal containing information that other actors possess about the focal firm, making it a crucial means through which quality can be communicated to prospective partners. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
