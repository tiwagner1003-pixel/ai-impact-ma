# Transaction Cost Economics

**A theory that explains the choice of governance structures (market, hybrid, hierarchy) by the costs — especially those arising from information asymmetry and opportunism — of executing transactions.**

## Summary

Transaction cost economics (TCE) was developed by Williamson (1975, 1985, 2005), building on Coase (1937). Its central insight is that bounded rationality, asset specificity, and opportunism under information asymmetry drive up transaction costs, making hierarchical governance (firm internalization) preferable to markets when these costs are high. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

Information asymmetry plays a distinctive role in TCE through the concept of "information impactedness" (Williamson 1975): the condition in which true circumstances relevant to a transaction are known to one or more parties but cannot be costlessly disclosed to or verified by others. Information impactedness raises search, monitoring, and bonding costs and creates conditions for opportunistic exploitation of the information advantage. The threat of opportunism forces uninformed parties to invest in safeguards — a cost that reduces efficiency. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

In M&A research, TCE is the second most frequently used theoretical perspective on information asymmetry (14% of 223 reviewed articles per Bergh et al. 2019). It underpins decisions about whether to acquire vs. ally, whether to use equity vs. asset acquisitions, and how to design contractual safeguards. Alliances can economize on information asymmetry-based transaction costs relative to full acquisitions, while acquisitions can resolve information impactedness by internalizing the information advantage. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]

## Variations / sub-concepts

- [[information-impactedness]] — the TCE-specific conceptualization of information asymmetry
- Asset specificity — the degree to which assets lose value outside a particular relationship; amplifies transaction costs
- Bounded rationality — cognitive limit on complete contracting; a precondition for information asymmetry to create costs
- Hold-up problem — post-investment opportunism enabled by asset specificity and information asymmetry

## Key claims across sources

- Transaction cost economics accounts for 14% of the 223 management articles reviewed by Bergh et al. (2019), making it the second most prominent theoretical lens after agency theory. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- Information impactedness raises search, monitoring, and bonding costs, and the proclivity to behave opportunistically when given the chance (Williamson 1975, per Bergh et al. 2019). [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- Alliances can economize on information asymmetry-based transaction costs by reducing continued search and bonding expenses while limiting specialized investment withdrawal costs associated with full hierarchical governance (Hennart 1993, per Bergh et al. 2019). [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
- When informational parity is costly for underinformed parties to achieve, firms with superior information may exploit it, causing "honest firms to leave the market" — the Akerlof lemons mechanism applied to inter-firm transactions. [[2026-05-07-bergh-et-al-2019-information-asymmetry-management-research]]
