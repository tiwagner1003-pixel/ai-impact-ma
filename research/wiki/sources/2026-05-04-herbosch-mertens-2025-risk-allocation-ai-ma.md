# The Future of Mergers & Acquisitions? Risk Allocation in AI-Guided Transactions

**Working-paper legal analysis of the comparative liability regime when AI tools make errors in M&A due diligence, arguing that across five jurisdictions existing legal standards already impose meaningful responsibilities on tool providers, service providers, boards of directors, and — as residual risk-bearers — the shareholders of acquiring companies.**

> Note: SSRN working paper (2025), not yet peer-reviewed. Authors are Maarten Herbosch (KU Leuven, Faculty of Law and Criminology) and Floris Mertens (Ghent University, PhD Candidate). URL: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5275610

## Key takeaways

- AI tools are inherently imperfect; erroneous AI output does not in itself constitute negligence by a provider, but it shifts legal responsibility to the human actors who selected, trained, and deployed the tool — with the selection choices and supervision practices being the decisive legal questions.
- A five-jurisdiction comparative analysis (German, French, English, Belgian, and Delaware/US law) shows that existing legal doctrines — duty of care, mistake doctrine, fiduciary duties, and the business judgment rule — already apply to AI-guided M&A without requiring fundamentally new regulatory frameworks.
- Flawed AI-generated information can, in principle, undermine the validity of an M&A agreement through the mistake doctrine (error on an essential contractual element), but numerous contractual safeguards (representations and warranties, risk allocation clauses) make this remedy practically difficult to invoke.
- Board members face internal liability when relying on AI tools: the key questions are whether they exercised appropriate oversight (reasonable selection, adequate testing, ongoing supervision), and whether they maintained meaningful human review — not whether the AI was correct.
- Explainability is a central legal safeguard: deploying an unexplainable AI model for an economically significant M&A decision risks being found grossly negligent in Delaware and in breach of the Ision-doctrine plausibility review in Germany, because the board cannot demonstrate the good-faith informational basis that both jurisdictions require.
- Residual risk from AI-related M&A errors ultimately falls on shareholders of acquiring companies, who have limited practical tools to limit this exposure ex ante.

## Claims

- As of 2025, 97% of Deloitte survey respondents report their companies and PE firms have used AI, data analytics, and automation for M&A due diligence, up from 69% in 2022. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Approximately 80% of M&A professionals anticipate overall AI adoption in the near future across all deal phases (citing Bain & Company 2024 survey). [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Today's M&A AI tools are primarily used for due diligence document review: detecting liabilities, flagging problematic contract clauses, and reducing information disadvantage for the acquirer. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- AI systems are inherently imperfect and unpredictable; their statistical "decision-making" process produces errors that do not resemble human mistakes and can be triggered by minor variations in input data. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- When an AI tool provider has explicitly guaranteed performance levels in the contract, the tool must meet that threshold; absent explicit guarantees, the general standard is fitness for intended use assessed via contextual cost-benefit analysis. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- When a service provider (e.g., law firm) deploys AI tools, the presence of AI does not alter the fundamental obligations of the service relationship; the service provider must select the tool with reasonable care, train it appropriately, and supervise its use. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- The validity of an M&A contract can, in principle, be challenged through the mistake doctrine if erroneous AI output caused an essential element of the contract to be misunderstood, but most legal systems require the mistake to be "excusable" — an assessment that depends on the sophistication of the relying party. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- When a seller deliberately provides incorrect data to an AI tool used by the buyer, this may facilitate fraudulent misrepresentation claims, making contract invalidation easier to achieve. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- The Delaware business judgment rule presumes that board AI use complies with procedural due care; gross negligence must be proven by plaintiff shareholders, which sets a board-friendly standard. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- The German business judgment rule does not presume care — the board must affirmatively demonstrate an appropriately informed basis and the absence of irresponsible risks, which is a stricter burden when AI was the information source. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- For economically significant M&A decisions, the board's informational duty requires cross-verification of AI output against other available information sources; sole reliance on an unexplainable AI constitutes blind reliance, which is impermissible across all five jurisdictions. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Explainability requirements serve two legal functions: they enable the board to detect false positives/negatives (supervision) and they allow the board to account for its informational basis if challenged in court (accountability). [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Belgium and the Netherlands apply "marginal review" of board decisions rather than a full business judgment rule, providing directors less structural protection; the procedure leading to a decision (including its AI information basis) is subject to full judicial review in those jurisdictions. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- Shareholders of acquiring companies bear the residual risk from AI-related M&A errors because (a) contractual terms typically burden the buyer's liability claims against providers, and (b) business judgment protections shield boards from most internal liability claims — leaving shareholders with limited remedies. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]
- The EU AI Act's high-risk AI system requirements are referenced as a useful benchmark for due diligence when selecting and testing AI tools, even though M&A due diligence AI systems likely classify as low-risk under the Act's current categories. [[2026-05-04-herbosch-mertens-2025-risk-allocation-ai-ma]]

## Notes

### Working-paper status
This is a 2025 SSRN working paper that has not yet appeared in a peer-reviewed journal. It should be cited with the SSRN identifier and flagged as forthcoming/working paper in the seminar bibliography. Given both authors are affiliated with Belgian law faculties, the Belgian-law treatment is the most original comparative contribution; the Delaware analysis largely follows established academic commentary.

### Key quotes worth preserving

From the conclusion (p. 43):
> "AI systems play an increasingly important role in facilitating all aspects of M&A transactions, particularly in the due diligence analysis that precedes such transactions. With that potential come significant risks. The inherently imperfect nature and distinct 'decision-making' of AI systems mean some outputs will be manifestly incorrect and conceptually inexplicable, potentially leading to undesirable transactions."

From the abstract:
> "The article concludes with best-practice recommendations to mitigate shareholder exposure, emphasizing AI system selection, supervision, and explainability as key legal safeguards."

From the introduction (p. 2):
> "A key characteristic of AI systems is that, like humans, they are inherently imperfect. The increasing use of AI thus entails a risk of erroneous output and potentially undesirable transactions. This shifts responsibility to the human actors, prompting assessments of how they selected and deployed the relevant AI tools."

### Structure of the paper
The paper has a clear three-part architecture:
1. **AI-Support of M&A Transactions** (pp. 2–8): AI definition (EU AI Act Article 3(1)), spectrum from "assisted" to "augmented" to "autonomous" intelligence, due diligence as dominant current application, benefits and inherent imperfections.
2. **External Implications of AI-Supported Transactions** (pp. 8–16): (a) Liability of tool providers — contractual performance standards, fitness-for-use, Hand formula cost-benefit; (b) Liability of deployers/service providers (law firms) — duty of best efforts, supervision and explainability; (c) M&A counterparty/transaction validity — mistake doctrine across five jurisdictions, fraudulent misrepresentation.
3. **Internal Implications of Board's AI Use** (pp. 16–37): Standard of conduct (accuracy of information basis, selection of AI system, supervision of AI system), standard of review (Delaware business judgment rule, German BJR, UK marginal review, Belgian/Netherlands marginal review).
4. **Bridging the Gap** (pp. 38–43): Comparison of external vs. internal regimes; risk allocation conclusion that shareholders bear residual risk.

### Open questions raised
- Does M&A due diligence AI (e.g., Kira Legal) actually qualify as "high-risk" under the EU AI Act Annex III? The paper assumes "low-risk" but notes exceptions.
- Has any M&A transaction actually been invalidated (or judicial challenge brought) on grounds of erroneous AI output? The paper is theoretical — empirical cases are lacking.
- What specific contractual clauses (reps and warranties, indemnities, MAC clauses) most effectively shift AI-risk away from shareholders? This is signalled but not fully mapped.
- Will AI governance frameworks evolve to require explicit AI disclosure in M&A transaction documentation (data rooms, board presentations)?

### Placement in seminar paper
This source belongs primarily in Abschnitt 4 (Kritische Reflexion): governance, liability, and legal risk. It directly answers the question "what happens when AI tools err in M&A?" and provides the most legally rigorous treatment of AI-governance obligations in M&A available in the prescribed reading list. The 97%/80% adoption statistics from pp. 1–2 are also useful for Einleitung/Grundlagenteil.
