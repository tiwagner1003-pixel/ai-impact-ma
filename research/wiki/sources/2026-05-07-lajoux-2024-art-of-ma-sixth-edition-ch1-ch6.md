# The Art of M&A (6th ed., 2024) — Chapters 1 & 6

**The sixth and final edition of the standard comprehensive M&A practitioner Q&A guide: Chapter 1 establishes the eight-phase M&A lifecycle (Strategy → Valuation → Financing → Structuring → Due Diligence → Negotiation → Closing → Integration) and key definitional terms; Chapter 6 provides the most comprehensive available practitioner treatment of the classical due diligence inquiry, covering legal foundations, VDR infrastructure, duration, DD levels, red flags, seller relations, location of research, asset evaluation, litigation analysis, and post-closing DD obligations.**

## Key takeaways

- The M&A process is best conceptualised as a circular eight-phase cycle; due diligence is Phase 5 ("verifying that the company is what it claims to be and discovering risk exposures material to the transaction") and is formally triggered by the signing of a confidentiality agreement and letter of intent.
- Due diligence begins well before the formal phase: in the Strategy, Valuation, Financing, and Structuring phases the acquirer already performs preliminary review using publicly available information (press reports, securities filings, offering memoranda).
- Classical DD comprises four complementary activity streams — financial statements review, management and operations review, legal compliance review, and document/transaction review — each requiring different professional expertise, all of which should communicate across organisational boundaries.
- DD duration spans from a few weeks to more than a year; fast-track DD is preferable because it minimises disruption, reduces cost, and rapidly signals deal viability, but parties may waive or shorten DD in exchange for broader representations and warranties.
- "Red flags" in DD are structured across four categories (financial, operational, liability/compliance, transactional) and require investigation even if the full picture is unclear; the due diligence defense under US securities law — particularly Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933 — depends on demonstrating a reasonable, affirmative investigation rather than passive reliance on management's representations.
- The book explicitly acknowledges AI and ChatGPT in the preface as a disruptive force reshaping how dealmakers formulate and research questions; Chapter 3 (Valuation) even includes a checklist for questions to ask about AI-embedded assets, signaling that AI integration is now a substantive DD topic in the 2024 edition.
- The sixth edition is described as the final volume in the McGraw-Hill series; the foreword by Jim Jeffries confirms the series conclusion and characterises the book as the definitive reference for both M&A professionals and students.

## Claims

- The M&A acquisition process follows seven recurring phases in circular order: Strategy, Valuation, Financing, Structuring, Due Diligence, Negotiation, Closing; Integration (postmerger) closes the loop back to Strategy. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Due diligence is formally defined as verifying that the company is what it claims to be and discovering risk exposures material to the transaction. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- "Merger" and "acquisition" are not interchangeable: "acquisition" is the generic term for any ownership transfer; "merger" is a narrow legal term for the specific statutory procedure where one corporation is absorbed into and disappears into another. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Hostile acquisitions occur in no more than one in ten public company deals; the vast majority of acquisitions are friendly or negotiated transactions. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- The due diligence process begins from the moment a buyer senses a possible acquisition opportunity, with formal DD triggered by signing of a confidentiality agreement and letter of intent. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- The four DD activity streams are: (1) financial statements review (existence of assets, liabilities, equity; quality of earnings); (2) management and operations review (leadership quality, internal controls, risks beyond financial statements); (3) legal compliance review (potential future legal problems from past/present operations); (4) document and transaction review (paperwork and structure of the deal). [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Outside counsel to the acquirer normally directs the overall DD review; the party directing the review should be free of conflicts of interest, including any firm with a financial relationship with or contingency fee tied to the target. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Cybersecurity is now a major DD risk: the review must cover IT infrastructure (firewalls, intrusion detection), cloud applications ("shadow IT"), software tools including email and document management, the IT/cybersecurity organisation, social media use, and data privacy compliance (GDPR). [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- A virtual data room (VDR) is the modern infrastructure for DD document storage and access; full-service VDRs include mobile apps, deal-phase-specific sharing platforms, indexing templates matching checklist categories, integration with document systems (e.g., Microsoft Office), encryption, and visual document tracking. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- The DD checklist should parallel the representations and warranties structure of the acquisition agreement; checklist items include thresholds of materiality (e.g., dollar limits for required documentation). [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- DD duration lasts from a few weeks to a year or more; speedy (fast-track) DD is preferred because it minimises disruption, reduces cost, and allows the buyer to quickly determine whether the deal is viable and on what terms. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- DD levels must be "reasonable but not perfect"; the degree of thoroughness depends on the companies involved (public vs. private), industry-specific risk exposures, deal size, and the seller's willingness to provide representations and warranties. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Under Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933, issuers are strictly liable for material misstatements or omissions in a registration statement (without requiring proof of scienter); the due diligence defense — showing a reasonable belief based on reasonable investigation — is the primary defense available to directors, officers, and underwriters. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- A 2023 US policy grants a safe harbor from prosecution to acquirers that discover legal violations during DD if they disclose the problems and make remediation. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Financial red flags include auditor resignations, accounting method changes, insider stock sales, and unusual financial ratios. Operational red flags include very high or very low turnover, weak quality controls, and poor safety records. Liability/compliance red flags include litigation exposure and inadequate legal compliance systems. Transactional red flags include conflicting accounting and tax goals. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- DD continues past signing through closing via a "bring-down" condition, requiring the seller to confirm no material adverse change has occurred; DD is properly understood to extend up to, through, and beyond closing. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- Cultural due diligence is explicitly required: the acquirer must assess values, team structures, and motivations; integration planning must begin as early as possible in the DD phase. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]
- The book explicitly references ChatGPT as reshaping how dealmakers formulate questions, and the 2024 edition includes a checklist for evaluating AI-embedded assets in target companies. [[2026-05-07-lajoux-2024-art-of-ma-sixth-edition-ch1-ch6]]

## Notes


**Sixth edition as the final volume:** The foreword by Jim Jeffries and the preface both confirm this is "the last of the series published by McGraw Hill." The edition captures M&A developments 2019–2024, including pandemic effects, AI adoption, and post-2023 M&A rebound.

**Key structural differences from Howson (2003):** Lajoux (2024) Chapter 6 takes a legal and securities-law grounding for DD that Howson (2003) does not: it is explicitly anchored in the Securities Act of 1933, the Exchange Act of 1934, and the case law on the "due diligence defense." Howson's framework is more project-management-oriented (five strands, 16 disciplines, adviser coordination). Both are complementary: Lajoux provides the legal/definitional foundation; Howson provides the operational management model.

**New in the 2024 edition:** Explicit cybersecurity DD (cloud, BYOD, GDPR), cultural DD (referenced to PMI planning), a reference to ChatGPT in the preface, and a Chapter 3 AI checklist for valuing AI assets — none of which appear in the 2019 preview. The virtual data room (VDR) infrastructure is also treated in significantly more detail.

**Citing in Harvard style:** Lajoux, A.R. (2024) *The Art of M&A: A Merger, Acquisition, and Buyout Guide*, 6th edn. New York: McGraw-Hill Education.

**Comparison with Howson (2003) and Bhagwan et al. (2018):** All three sources converge on the breadth of DD disciplines. Lajoux (2024) adds a legal-compliance anchoring and explicit technology/cybersecurity dimensions. Bhagwan et al. (2018) provides the systematic-review evidence base for the same discipline list. Howson (2003) provides the project-management governance model.
